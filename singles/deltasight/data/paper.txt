Abstract
The high energy cost of video AIoT systems stems from redun-
dant operations across sensing, transmission, and computation.
While prior work optimizes individual stages, the lack of cross-stage
coordination forces each stage to re-detect redundancy, limiting
overall efficiency. We present DeltaSight, an algorithm–hardware
co-design architecture that establishes a sensor-side unified re-
dundancy criterion serving as the common basis for redundancy
elimination throughout the pipeline. Algorithmically, we gener-
ate this criterion via sensor-side block-level redundancy detection
whose output matches the granularity of downstream computation,
complemented by a semantic-aware sampling strategy that adapts
precision to task relevance. An architecture is designed to support
this algorithm with minimal hardware additions. DeltaSight gains
2.4× sensor-side and 1.7× end-to-end energy efficiency, with slight
accuracy improvements.

As shown in Fig. 1, a typical end-to-end vision system generally
comprises three stages: (i) sensing, where sampling is performed
at a fixed frame rate and full resolution, followed by analog-to-
digital conversion (ADC) and image signal processing (ISP); (ii)
transmission, where the processed data are delivered to the host via
a serial interface such as MIPI; and (iii) computation, where neural
models (e.g., CNNs [5] or transformers [23]) execute inference tasks
such as detection or other decision making in host side.
To reduce video AIoT system energy consumption, recent studies
have leveraged the inherent temporal similarity in video streams
to design redundancy-elimination algorithms [10, 17, 24] and cus-
tomized accelerators [12, 19, 20], achieving substantial energy sav-
ings at the host side. Building upon this observation, our experimen-
tal results further verify that, as shown in Fig. 2, even eliminating
over 60% of the data and their associated computations causes neg-
ligible degradation in model accuracy. This observation suggests that
a large fraction of sensing and transmission effort is also redundant.
Worse still, recent surveys on advanced AIoT systems [3, 8] re-
veal that the often-overlooked sensing and transmission stages ac-
count for as much as 37%∼53% of total system energy consumption.

Although prior efforts have explored compressed acquisition [25]
and adaptive sensing [7, 22] to lower sampling and ADC costs, or
pushed lightweight pre-processing to the sensor side to alleviate
transmission overhead [9], these methods remain detached from
host-side demands. Their designs primarily target image fidelity
rather than task efficiency, overlooking the fact that AIoT sensing
ultimately serves intelligent computation rather than for human
viewing. Due to the lack of systematic consideration, the potential
for system-level energy reduction remains fundamentally limited.
Therefore, we propose DeltaSight, a cross-stage co-optimized
video AIoT design paradigm that unifies the granularities of sensing,
transmission, and computation to achieve consistent redundancy
elimination. DeltaSight adopts a hardware–algorithm co-design
approach. Algorithmically, it introduces a block-level redundancy
identification algorithm designed for sensor-side to localize tem-
poral redundancy at the source, and leverages semantic priors to
estimate regional importance and guide task-aware mixed-precision
quantization. Architecturally, it extends a conventional SAR ADC
with lightweight, reconfigurable support for inter-frame compari-
son and A/D conversion, complemented by a block-parallel readout
circuit that aligns sensing and computation granularities, and a
neighborhood-aware dynamic ADC scheduling mechanism that
mitigates load imbalance caused by redundancy.
In summary, we have the following contributions:
• We propose DeltaSight, a cross-stage co-optimized video
AIoT system that unifies sensing, transmission, and compu-
tation granularities for consistent redundancy elimination.
• At the algorithm level, we introduce sensor-side patch-level
similarity identification and region-importance awareness
aligned with the host’s granularity, establishing a unified
criterion for consistent cross-stage redundancy elimination.
• We enable sensor-side similarity identification and mixed-
precision A/D conversion with minimal hardware overhead
by lightly extending the existing sensor architecture.
• Extensive evaluations demonstrate 2.4× sensor-side and 1.7×
video AIoT system efficiency gains, accompanied by modest
accuracy improvements.

2.3
Motivations
Motivation 1: Computational Redundancy Revealing Unnec-
essary Sensing and Transmission. Continuous video streams
inherently exhibit strong temporal correlations, where a large pro-
portion of pixels or regions remain unchanged between adjacent
frames. As shown in Fig. 2(a), we eliminate redundant computations
in the model by reusing intermediate features of similar regions
across adjacent frames. Results indicate that even after removing
over 60% of the data and corresponding computations, the inference
accuracy remains nearly unchanged. This motivates us that most of
the data sensing and transmission are unnecessary.
Although several recent sensor designs [13, 22] exploit temporal
similarity to reduce redundant sampling, they make decisions at
the pixel level, while downstream models operate at the patch level.
This granularity mismatch hinders full redundancy elimination and
provides no patch-level guidance for sparse execution, requiring
the host to re-identify redundant regions on its own.
Motivation 2: Spatial Noise Sensitivity Revealing the Limita-
tions of Fixed-Precision Sensing. Conventional sensing pipelines
quantize all pixels with identical precision, assuming that all spatial
regions contribute equally to downstream accuracy. To examine
this assumption, we conduct a controlled perturbation study on the
NFS dataset by injecting identical noise into target and non-target
regions. As shown in Fig. 2(b), the results exhibit a asymmetry:
corrupting foreground regions leads to a noticeable accuracy drop,
while adding noise to background areas within a reasonable range
produces minimal impact and can even leave accuracy unchanged.
This motivates us to adopt dynamic sampling strategies based on
semantic importance to further reduce sensor-side energy.

Architecture Overview
System Overview. Fig. 5(a) illustrates the system architecture of
DeltaSight, which consists of a sensor connected to the host pro-
cessor via a MIPI CSI interface. Fig. 4 presents the system timing
diagram of DeltaSight. Compared with conventional video process-
ing systems, DeltaSight exhibits three key distinctions in its archi-
tecture and operational mechanism: (i) It incorporates patch-level
similarity identification and semantic awareness mechanisms, and
divides the A/D conversion into high- and low-bit stages (shown
in blue), enabling the filtering of most pixel-level conversions and
supporting mixed-precision conversion to accommodate the vary-
ing precision requirements of regions with different importance;
(ii) An inter-frame dependency is introduced: the inference result
of the previous frame 𝐹𝑡−1 is fed back to the sensor through the
MIPI CSI interface to support the semantic awareness process of the
current frame 𝐹𝑡; (iii) An intra-frame dependency also exists, where
patch-level similarity information guides sparse transmission and
computation between the sensor and the host.

4.2
Sensor Design
4.2.1
Design of Reconfigurable ADC. The design objective of the
reconfigurable ADC is to ensure the fundamental A/D conversion
functionality while maximizing the reuse of existing hardware
resources to enable the proposed inter-frame similarity judgment
algorithm. To compute the similarity between pixels in adjacent
frames, two key issues must be addressed: (i) how to retain the
analog pixel voltage of the previous frame, and (ii) how to compare
the voltage difference between two corresponding pixels.
For (i), prior works [3, 8] rely on nonvolatile or capacitive stor-
age, which either increases hardware cost or introduces thermal
noise and drift. Instead, we exploit the deterministic D/A mapping
of the CDAC in a standard SAR ADC: previous-frame pixel values
are kept as digital codes in on-chip SRAM, and their analog volt-
ages are reconstructed during comparison, eliminating the need for
dedicated holding circuits.
For (ii), we introduce an extremely small capacitor 𝐶𝜏(high-
lighted in red in Fig. 5(c)), which is added to the top-plate node of the
existing CDAC within the SAR ADC. This modification leverages
the original comparison path, including the built-in comparator and
CDAC array, to realize symmetric ±𝜏voltage injection by switching
among existing voltage nodes; if the two successive comparison re-
sults differ, the inter-frame voltage difference at this pixel is within
the threshold and thus judged similar, otherwise dissimilar.
By comparing the two successive outcomes under the +𝜏and
−𝜏injections, the inter-frame voltage difference can be inferred:
identical results imply a difference beyond the threshold (dissimilar),
while opposite results indicate it falls within the threshold (similar).
Since𝐶𝜏is much smaller than the main CDAC array (approximately
0.5% of the total capacitance in our design), the induced voltage
disturbance remains far below the effective decision step of the
current stage and thus does not affect threshold determination.

Design of Block-level Readout Circuit. We adopt a block-level
execution mode that exposes block-level control to the A/D path.
Under this execution model, the mixed-precision pipeline allocates
0/4/10-bit quantization driven by similarity and semantics.
Block-Level Readout Circuit. As shown in Fig. 7, we adopt a
block-level readout scheme featuring row concurrency, column
parallelism, and dual-block shared ADC arrays. The pixel array op-
erates at a minimum execution granularity of 𝑃× 𝑃(with 𝑃= 16 in
this design). Every two adjacent blocks in the column direction form
a 16× 32 readout domain, which corresponds to one group of recon-
figurable ADC arrays. Each ADC array contains 16 column-parallel
channels, operating under a unified sampling clock. The controller
uses the block-select (blk_sel) signal to control 16 2:1 MUXs within
each domain to select the active block. The MUX outputs are then
connected to the ADC inputs through their corresponding column
enable switches (col_en). The controller issues col_en bitmasks at
the pixel level to achieve column-wise gating and sparse sampling(1:

4.3
Cooperation with Transmission and Host
This section briefly introduces how redundancy-aware results are
used to guide sparse transmission and enable sparse computation.
Guide for Sparse Transmission. After sampling all blocks of
the current frame, the in-sensor NPU performs local Bayer-to-RGB
demosaicing only on image blocks within the non-similarity set.
These blocks are then reorganized into a single logical frame using
the Multi-ROI coalesced-frame mechanism, whose header records
each updated block’s ID. The host retains the complete previous
frame and reconstructs the current frame by directly replacing the
corresponding regions according to these indices. The entire process
remains fully compliant with the MIPI CSI-2 physical layer, while
extending only the protocol layer to support block-level differential
updates and semantic indexing, thereby enabling efficient and low-
redundancy incremental image transmission.
Guide for Sparse Computation. On the host side, we bypass any
additional redundancy analysis and directly consume the sensor-
provided region-level similarity map as the execution mask. Prior
work [2, 17, 19, 24] has provided mechanisms that leverage tem-
poral similarity for feature reuse and sparse transformer execu-
tion. Following this paradigm, our system uses the change map
to gate patch-level computation: updated regions are recomputed,
while cached features for unchanged regions are fetched from on-
chip memory and forwarded. This enables seamless temporal reuse
across frames without introducing extra host-side logic.
5
Evaluation Results
5.1
Experimental Setup
Hardware Implementation. The proposed system comprises a
sensor and a host processor connected via a MIPI interface. The
sensor modules are described in Verilog/Verilog-A and synthesized
using Synopsys Design Compiler targeting the TSMC 65 nm pro-
cess. Switching activities are annotated and analyzed in Synopsys
PrimeTime PX to estimate both dynamic and leakage power, while
on-chip memory energy is modeled using CACTI [21] and DRAM
energy is derived from Ramulator command traces. The host proces-
sor adopts the transformer accelerator CMC [19], which supports
temporal feature reuse, with its similarity search unit removed; we
claim no innovation in this part.
Benchmarks and Software Implementation. We evaluate DeltaSight
using three transformer models: ViTDeT-S (384-dim), ViTDeT-B
(672-dim), and ViTDeT-L (1024-dim) [11]. Experiments are con-
ducted on two standard video detection datasets, ImageNet VID
[18] and NFS [4]. All models adopt a 16×16 patch size and are im-
plemented in PyTorch [16] for baseline comparison. We use [1] to
invert the RGB image back to its analog Bayer representation.
AIoT System Variants. To quantify the contribution of each sub-
system to the overall energy of a video AIoT pipeline, we evaluate
three system variants:
• Base-Full: Represents a conventional video AIoT pipeline
that performs full sensing, transmission, and computation.
• Pixel-E: Represents a variant that performs pixel-level re-
dundancy elimination directly on the sensor side.

Effects of Mixed-Precision A/D Conversion. Table 2 reports
the bit-width distribution under our mixed-precision scheme. Over
79.4% of pixels are assigned 0-bit and thus skip conversion, while
only 10.5%∼18.1% require full 10-bit conversion. This skewed distri-
bution lowers A/D conversion energy by 83.9% on NFS and 74.2%
on VID, compared with conventional fixed-precision sampling. The
gains stem from three factors: (i) similar blocks are fully exempt
from conversion, (ii) within similar blocks, pixel-level similarity
further suppresses conversions, and (iii) in low-importance regions,
similar pixels undergo only partial conversion.

Sensor Area Analysis. While post-layout simulations provide
accurate power and timing, the silicon area is estimated from the
array footprint and the composition of bottom-tier circuits. Our
stacked design integrates a 672×672 5T pixel array whose cell ge-
ometry follows the design [6] with a 2.9 𝜇m pixel pitch; together
with the aligned block readout and reconfigurable ADCs, this region
occupies 6.81 mm2. The ADC arrays reside entirely beneath the
array and therefore introduce no outline overhead. The remaining
non-aligned blocks, including the SRAM (0.9 MB), global timing and
control circuits, in-sensor NPU, and the peripheral I/O circuitry, are
placed outside the active array and add roughly 0.6 mm2 in 65-nm
CMOS. The total die area is therefore approximately 7.4 mm2.
6
Conclusion
We presented DeltaSight, a video AIoT system that achieves consis-
tent redundancy elimination across the processing pipeline. Through
sensor-side similarity detection and importance-aware mixed-precision
sensing, DeltaSight suppresses unnecessary sampling, computation,
and data movement while imposing only minimal hardware over-
head. To the best of our knowledge, DeltaSight is the first video
AIoT system that unifies the granularities of sensing, transmis-
sion, and computation, enabling a coherent execution paradigm for
cross-stage redundancy handling.
